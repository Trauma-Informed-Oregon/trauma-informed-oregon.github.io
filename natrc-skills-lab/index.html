How the breakouts work

Join your assigned group. Speak or use the group chat. Cameras on is encouraged. Each group works on the same case. If you need access or technical assistance, contact the host privately. This session will not be recorded.

For each case, say the response aloud, discuss what it depends on, and revise it together. Bring back one response your group can explain.

Breakout 1 · 15 minutes

Alex

Protected documentation time

The situation

Alex is an experienced staff member. Six records from the past two weeks remain incomplete, and colleagues lack information needed for follow-up. The supervisor and Alex previously agreed on protected documentation time. Alex says, “Every time I start, I’m asked to cover outreach.”

The scheduling record shows that a manager reassigned Alex during three protected blocks. Two colleagues have been completing overflow work after hours. The missing information still needs attention.

Your group’s task

  1. What would you say?Draft an opening and one question whose answer could change your decision.
  2. What would you do?Name the expectation, the support and who owns the next step.
  3. Test your response.Say it aloud. Colleagues give feedback, then revise it together. Bring one response back.

Questions to test your response

  • What remains Alex’s responsibility? What must the supervisor change?
  • How will colleagues receive the information they need without absorbing more unplanned work?
  • How might the supervisor’s urgency affect the conversation?

Breakout 2 · 20 minutes

Devin

Growing work, unchanged target

The situation

Devin coordinates outreach and also orients new staff, repairs strained community relationships, and helps colleagues adapt materials for language access. The original productivity target remains unchanged. Devin says, “I keep being evaluated as though none of this extra work exists.”

The supervisor forwards extra requests because Devin is trusted and usually says yes. Another colleague previously challenged similar assignments and received fewer extra requests. The program director sets the target. The supervisor can pause new orientation assignments this week and bring a workload proposal to the director Friday.

Devin worries that meeting the target will mean cutting relationship and language-access work that makes services reachable. Devin has become quieter in supervision and appears distracted. The cause is unknown. The supervisor needs a workable plan for this week and a decision about the ongoing workload.

An ethical question in this case

Which commitment to access is at risk if the target remains unchanged? What can the supervisor decide now, and what must the director review? Naming the concern should lead to a decision about work and resources.

Research with healthcare workers links moral injury to organizational culture and workplace conditions that can change (Purcell et al., 2024). A difficult decision alone does not establish moral injury.

Your group’s task

  1. What would you say?Draft an opening and one question whose answer could change your decision.
  2. What would you do?Name the expectation, the support and who owns the next step.
  3. Test your response.Say it aloud. Colleagues give feedback, then revise it together. Bring one response back.

Questions to test your response

  • What commitment is Devin concerned about losing?
  • What can the supervisor change now, and what requires the director’s decision?
  • How would you respond to withdrawal without assuming its cause?
  • What will you tell Devin while the decision is pending, including if the request is declined?

Keep for your next conversation

Accountability with compassion

Name the work and its purpose. Check whether the agreed support was available. Clarify what is required and where the person has meaningful choice.

Reflective listening

Put the concern into words the person can correct. Listen to the correction before offering an explanation or solution.

Strategic questions

Ask for information that could change the decision. “If the priority were clear, would there be enough time?” distinguishes ambiguity from a capacity problem.

Recognizing possible stress responses

Notice changes and ask about the current work. Offer a manageable pace or written follow-up. Workload, exposure to others’ trauma, and ethical strain may need different responses. Personal disclosure is not required.

Maintaining your capacity

Notice your own urgency, irritation or impulse to take over. Promise support you can deliver. Seek consultation and take unresolved decisions to someone with authority.

Parallel process

A supervisor receiving pressure may pass it along by tightening control or removing choice. Ask: “What am I repeating, and what can I change?” Acknowledge your contribution, clarify priorities and follow through.

Prepare your own response

Choose an upcoming supervisory conversation. Keep your notes private.

  • Opening: What happened, and why does it need attention?
  • Reflection: What concern can you put into words for the person to correct?
  • Question: What answer could change your decision?
  • Action: What will each person do, and what support can you deliver?
  • Follow-up: When will you review the work and the promised support?
